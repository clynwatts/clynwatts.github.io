I study the role groundwater plays in freshwater wetland restorations in former cranberry bogs in southeastern Massachusetts. In conjunction with collaborators at [Living Observatory](https://projects.livingobservatory.org/people/lwatts), we are answering questions around environmental restoration impacts these sites. I investigate how water moves through these wetlands, and how flowpaths change in response to restoration and changing climate. I use a wide variety of tools to answer these questions, including isotope geochemistry, fiber-optic distributed temperature sensing, and thermal drone mapping. Broadly, my interests include: wetland hydrology, flood hazard mitigation, temperature as a groundwater tracer, drones as geoscience tools. Always, I seek projects that are community driven or address a community need.

## The hydrologic evolution of restored wetlands
**Why wetlands?** Since 1700, an estimated 3.4 million km2 (about 21%) of global wetlands have been destroyed to allow the development of “usable” land. Now we understand that wetlands provide many benefits, including both flood and drought mitigation, carbon sequestration, and habitat refuge for plants, animals, and people. In the United Nations’ decade of “Ecosystem Restoration”, governments, non-profits, and private agencies are engaged in the restoration of various types of degraded environments globally. Wetlands are being restored to return some of the benefits which were lost. 

We are asking how the process of restoration changes the hydrologic regime, and the role groundwater seeps play in building a functioning wetland ecosystem. 

## Atlantic white cedar swamps: Hydrologic drivers of tree regeneration
As an outgrowth of our work on cranberry bog wetland restorations, we are collaborating with a group of stakeholders (including land trusts, state agencies, environmental nonprofits, and indigenous orgs) who are working to restore and manage Atlantic white cedar swamps. Atlantic white cedars (AWC) are a wetland tree species which grows in kettlehole peatlands. Many of the sites that are part of my current research were built on sites that were once cedar
swamps, and these swamps are now a threatened ecosystem. We need to understand land
management history (by collecting soil cores) and the hydrologic regime that allows these trees
to survive. This research will inform wetland forest management practices and restoration work
of these ecosystems along the east coast.
